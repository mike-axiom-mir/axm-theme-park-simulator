2. INSPIRATION WITHOUT CLONING
======================================================================

The remembered PlayStation 1 game was:

THEME PARK WORLD
North-American title: SIM THEME PARK

What worked:

- a solid tycoon foundation;
- strongly separated themed worlds;
- the ability to enter and experience the park;
- the ability to ride attractions;
- a feeling that the built park was a place, not only a spreadsheet;
- limited graphics that still produced strong atmosphere and imagination.

Important comparison:

ROLLERCOASTER TYCOON contributed:

- readable construction;
- clear park operations;
- understandable building logic;
- strong classic tycoon foundations.

THEME PARK WORLD contributed:

- personal adventure;
- first-person park presence;
- emotional access to what the player built;
- themed-world atmosphere.

MODERN PARK BUILDERS contributed:

- larger construction freedom;
- visual range;
- complex sandbox tools.

The project must not copy one existing game. It takes proven strengths, rejects
weak conventions, and builds a larger causal experience on top.

Core experiential loop:

DESIGN
→ OPERATE
→ ENTER THE PARK
→ PERSONALLY EXPERIENCE OR INSPECT IT
→ NOTICE PROBLEMS OR ATMOSPHERE
→ RETURN TO MANAGEMENT
→ EVOLVE IT

3. PRIMARY REJECTED CONVENTION
======================================================================

Rejected model:

- build a ride;
- it is popular because it is new;
- use causes an arbitrary hidden decay;
- popularity drops because a timer says so;
- player must delete and rebuild effectively the same thing;
- the game manufactures activity by destroying player history.

This was identified as annoying, shallow, and unrealistic.

The intended alternative:

A player should grow and think, not repeatedly delete and rebuild because the
simulation requires artificial churn.

A park element never loses value merely because it is old.

Its value changes because of:

- people;
- geography;
- awareness;
- prior exposure;
- repeat strength;
- expectations;
- maintenance;
- reliability;
- operations;
- capacity;
- crowding;
- theme and atmosphere;
- comfort;
- accessibility;
- park identity;
- competition;
- cost;
- land pressure;
- weather;
- seasonality;
- cultural relevance;
- nostalgia;
- surrounding experiences;
- direct and indirect financial contribution.

======================================================================
4. CORE DESIGN RULE
======================================================================

EVERY NUMBER MUST REPRESENT A CAUSE,
NOT MERELY FORCE A GAMEPLAY OUTCOME.

Forbidden example:

"Ride is one year old, therefore popularity drops by 40%."

Acceptable explanation:

- nearby repeat visitors have high prior exposure;
- the local first-time market is becoming saturated;
- the ride has weak repeat value for that audience;
- marketing still targets the same local group;
- transport reach has not expanded;
- therefore demand from that specific segment is lower.

This does not mean the ride has become universally unpopular.

A tourist who has never visited may still experience it as completely new.

======================================================================
5. NO SINGLE GLOBAL POPULARITY BAR
======================================================================

Popularity must not exist as one universal property of a ride, shop, land, or
park.

Demand must be evaluated by context.

Relevant context includes:

- audience segment;
- origin geography;
- previous visits;
- individual or group preferences;
- budget;
- travel friction;
- current awareness;
- marketing message;
- expectation;
- weather;
- time of day;
- queue length;
- park crowding;
- service quality;
- group composition;
- accessibility needs;
- park identity;
- competing alternatives.

Novelty belongs to a person or audience, not to a building.

A twenty-year-old attraction can be:

- familiar to locals;
- new to tourists;
- nostalgic to older visitors;
- newly relevant to families;
- technically interesting to enthusiasts;
- valuable as a park landmark;
- financially strong because it is reliable and paid off.

======================================================================
6. MAINTAIN, EVOLVE, REPLACE
======================================================================

The player should have three fundamentally different choices.

MAINTAIN
Preserve what already works through cleaning, staffing, inspection, repair,
parts, painting, training, and operational care.

EVOLVE
Improve the attraction or area through:

- capacity changes;
- queue redesign;
- accessibility;
- better operations;
- new story;
- environmental changes;
- scenery;
- lighting;
- sound;
- seasonal overlays;
- refurbishment;
- targeted marketing;
- supporting nearby attractions;
- new transport links;
- new audience strategy.

REPLACE
Remove or replace something because the player intentionally chooses a better
future use of money, land, identity, or capacity.

Replacement may be sensible when:

- maintenance becomes structurally excessive;
- parts become difficult or expensive;
- the attraction no longer fits the chosen park identity;
- serious design flaws remain;
- land has a better strategic use;
- demand is insufficient across all relevant segments;
- the park chooses a deliberate new direction.

Replacement must not be routine forced upkeep.

======================================================================
7. AUDIENCE AND GEOGRAPHIC SIMULATION
======================================================================

The park must simulate a reachable audience rather than spawning generic guests.

Possible geographic groups:

- nearby residents;
- regional day visitors;
- domestic tourists;
- international tourists;
- hotel guests;
- school groups;
- enthusiasts travelling for specific attractions;
- seasonal-event visitors;
- returning local families;
- price-sensitive visitors;
- social groups.

A small local park may depend heavily on repeat visitation.

A destination park may continue to receive first-time visitors because it has a
larger geographic reach.

Reach should depend on:

- transport;
- roads;
- rail;
- parking;
- accommodation;
- reputation;
- marketing;
- ticket price;
- park identity;
- travel time;
- visitor budget;
- season;
- competing parks;
- destination strength.

Audience states may include:

- unaware;
- aware but uninterested;
- considering;
- first-time visitor;
- previous visitor;
- loyal repeat visitor;
- disappointed former visitor.

======================================================================
8. FIRST-EXPERIENCE AND REPEAT-EXPERIENCE VALUE
======================================================================

Attractions should expose at least two different experience dimensions:

FIRST-EXPERIENCE VALUE
How powerful, surprising, understandable, or memorable is the first experience?

REPEAT-EXPERIENCE VALUE
Why would someone choose to experience it again?

Repeat strength may come from:

- enjoyable sensation;
- different seats;
- changing outcomes;
- interactive elements;
- social enjoyment;
- short queues;
- variable weather or lighting;
- day/night differences;
- seasonal overlays;
- nostalgia;
- ritual;
- theme;
- personal memories;
- complementary nearby experiences.

An attraction may be excellent once and weak for repetition.

Another may be simple but remain enjoyable for decades.

======================================================================
9. PLAYER INTENT AND MULTIPLE VIABLE PARK IDENTITIES
======================================================================

The simulation should know what kind of park the player wants to create.

Possible identities:

- small local family park;
- regional day park;
- enthusiast coaster park;
- heavily themed destination park;
- historic preservation park;
- seasonal festival park;
- compact city park;
- resort park;
- mixed general-audience park;
- experimental adventure park.

The system should diagnose trade-offs against the chosen direction.

It must not force every park toward one universal optimization target.

A lightly themed coaster park can be successful.

A smaller park with modest rides but exceptional atmosphere can also be
successful.

A historic park can preserve older rides as landmarks.

A family park can succeed through comfort, reliability, variety, and value.

======================================================================
10. THEMING, SCENERY, AND ATMOSPHERE
======================================================================

Theming must be optional but genuinely influential.

Wrong model:

"Place 300 objects near a ride to earn +300 theme score."

Intended model:

The influence of scenery and theme depends on:

- thematic coherence;
- visual coverage;
- sightlines;
- queue integration;
- ride integration;
- land identity;
- transitions between areas;
- contradiction;
- clutter;
- lighting;
- sound;
- landscaping;
- environmental comfort;
- shade;
- cleanliness;
- maintenance;
- audience fit;
- story consistency;
- sensory integration;
- whether off-theme infrastructure is visible.

A plain coaster may provide:

- strong thrill;
- low atmosphere;
- low surrounding dwell time.

The same coaster inside a coherent themed land may provide:

- strong thrill;
- anticipation;
- a better queue;
- exploration value;
- stronger memory;
- wider audience appeal;
- a clearer park identity;
- more nearby spending;
- stronger repeat or recommendation potential.

Theming does not magically change the ride physics.

It changes the total experience around the ride.

======================================================================
11. MARKETING AND EXPECTATION
======================================================================

Marketing does not generate generic extra guests.

Marketing attracts particular audiences and creates expectations.

Examples:

- "fastest coaster in the region" attracts thrill seekers;
- "enchanted forest" attracts fantasy, atmosphere, and family audiences;
- "affordable local family day" attracts local value-conscious groups;
- Halloween marketing attracts seasonal and social visitors;
- nostalgia campaigns reactivate former visitors;
- hotel packages attract destination visitors.

Marketing creates a promise.

If the park delivers, trust and reputation can grow.

If the park fails to deliver, the attracted audience may be disappointed.

======================================================================
12. WHOLE-PARK CONTRIBUTION
======================================================================

An attraction or service should not be judged only by direct revenue.

Possible contribution dimensions:

- direct revenue;
- induced food and shop spending;
- attracting visits;
- extending visit duration;
- balancing crowds;
- increasing ticket willingness;
- completing a themed land;
- serving families;
- serving younger or older guests;
- serving accessibility needs;
- creating park identity;
- supporting reputation;
- creating nostalgia;
- encouraging return visits;
- preventing early departure;
- filling low-demand time periods.

A quiet family ride may look weak in isolation but keep families in the park
longer and improve the total day.

A landmark ride may justify its cost through ticket demand and park identity.

A low-cost classic may remain highly efficient inside a small park.

======================================================================
13. ACTIVE ATTENDANCE AND PARK SCALE
======================================================================

The number of visitors simultaneously inside the park matters.

The system should connect:

- daily attendance;
- peak active attendance;
- attraction throughput;
- operating hours;
- opening schedules;
- staffing;
- park size;
- path distances;
- queues;
- service capacity;
- food capacity;
- maintenance cost;
- crowd atmosphere;
- empty-space atmosphere;
- weather;
- seasonality.

A large park with too few active visitors may feel empty and cost too much.

A small park with too many visitors may become crowded and unpleasant.

An expensive high-capacity attraction may work in a destination park and fail
in a small local park.

A reliable inexpensive ride may be perfect for the smaller park.

======================================================================
14. BEGINNER LAYER
======================================================================

The deep simulation should remain active for every player.

A beginner layer should translate evidence into understandable guidance.

Example:

"The Dragon Coaster is mechanically healthy. Local thrill seekers have already
ridden it often, but families and tourists still enjoy it. Consider adding a
second thrill ride or targeting new visitors instead of replacing this one."

The beginner layer may:

- summarize;
- explain;
- warn;
- suggest;
- show likely trade-offs.

It may not replace the real simulation with fake simplified rules.

Deep underneath.
Humane and simple on top.

======================================================================
15. CAMPAIGN ADVENTURE
======================================================================

Campaigns should be living park problems, not only checklist objectives.

A campaign begins with:

- geography;
- existing assets;
- local population;
- transport;
- history;
- reputation;
- obligations;
- money;
- debts;
- staff;
- audience;
- land;
- regulations;
- unresolved tensions.

Example campaigns:

THE FORGOTTEN LOCAL PARK
An aging local park with loyal older visitors and little money.
The player must decide what to preserve, modernize, or retarget.

THE UNFINISHED FANTASY PARK
Beautiful scenery, broken operations, queues, and maintenance.
The player must make the dream function.

THE COASTER FIELD
Excellent rides, minimal comfort, theming, or family appeal.
The player may remain enthusiast-focused or broaden the park.

THE REMOTE DESTINATION
Cheap land and beautiful surroundings, but little nearby population.
Transport, hotels, and strong identity matter.

THE CROWDED CITY PARK
High demand, little land, noise limits, and capacity pressure.

THE HISTORICAL PARK
Protected attractions and landmarks must be integrated into a viable future.

Multiple endings should be valid.

The player does not conquer a map.

The player inherits a place, understands it, chooses what it should become, and
lives with the consequences.

16. MANAGEMENT AND ADVENTURE SHARE ONE WORLD
======================================================================

Adventure mode and management mode must use the same authoritative simulation.

Management view shows:

- what is happening;
- metrics;
- operations;
- evidence;
- system state.

Adventure view helps the player understand:

- why the place feels as it does;
- where visual contradictions exist;
- why queues are unpleasant;
- how weather changes an area;
- how a ride feels;
- how guests move;
- where staff struggle;
- how atmosphere succeeds or fails.

Adventure mode must not be a fake decorative copy of the park.

18. AUTHORITATIVE EVENT FLOW
======================================================================

1. A player, AI, campaign, or world process proposes an action.
2. The owning module validates the action.
3. The foundation records an ordered event with seed and evidence references.
4. Affected domain modules calculate state changes.
5. The foundation stores the result and explanation packet.
6. Interfaces render the same state at different levels of detail.

Deterministic replay rule:

Given the same initial state, ordered actions, module versions, and seed, the
simulation must reproduce the same transitions.

26. THE ALIEN PARK
======================================================================

If the player accepts, they travel with the alien into space.

The player arrives at a small alien theme park.

It must not use Earth assumptions with alien skins.

The alien park should include:

- unfamiliar species;
- unfamiliar senses;
- different movement;
- different social behavior;
- different time cycles;
- different environmental needs;
- different concepts of comfort;
- different risk perception;
- unfamiliar value exchange;
- attractions with unclear purpose;
- incomplete translation;

The player has learned Earth park logic.

Now the player must reason about an audience they do not understand.

======================================================================
27. OBSERVATION AS THE MAIN SPACE MECHANIC
======================================================================

The player must learn through structured experimentation.

Loop:

1. Build, activate, or modify something.
2. Observe which beings approach.
3. Observe behavior before, during, and after.
4. Compare reactions across species or groups.
5. Form a hypothesis.
6. Change one variable.
7. Test again.
8. Increase confidence.
9. Gradually unlock reliable understanding.

The interface should initially report patterns, not definitive answers.

Example:

"Repeated clustering detected near structures emitting wavelength pattern 7."

Later:

"Probable attraction to wavelength pattern 7.
Confidence: 63%.
Alternative cause: nearby thermal field."

This makes the player reason instead of following disguised objective markers.

======================================================================
28. UNKNOWN DOES NOT MEAN RANDOM
======================================================================

Space-campaign root:

UNKNOWN DOES NOT MEAN IRRATIONAL.
IT MEANS THE CAUSES HAVE NOT YET BEEN UNDERSTOOD.

The player must avoid:

- assuming alien behavior is random;
- assuming aliens are humans with strange graphics;
- forcing human values onto unknown intelligence;
- treating incomplete understanding as certainty.

This mirrors the deeper AXM challenge through entertainment rather than lecture.

29. EXAMPLES OF GENUINELY ALIEN PARK LOGIC
======================================================================

Possible civilizations:

- a species that values synchronized group experiences over individual rides;
- a species that experiences gravity changes as relaxation;
- a species that dislikes visual scenery but values magnetic rhythm;
- a species that uses vibration and temperature as atmosphere;
- a species that treats queues as social rituals;
- a species that visits attractions to produce knowledge;
- a collective species that visits parks to experience individuality;
- a species for which repetition creates trust rather than boredom;
- a species that follows moving gravitational paths instead of fixed walkways.

Possible campaigns:

THE SILENT FAIR
Translation claims the visitors are bored.
Observation reveals uncontrolled sound overwhelms them.

THE EMPTY ORBIT
The park appears inaccessible.
The visitors navigate through moving gravitational routes rather than fixed
destinations.

THE PERFECT RIDE
An expensive ride receives no visitors because it produces an identical result
every cycle, while the audience values variation and discovery.

======================================================================
30. SCIENCE-FICTION DLC ROOT
======================================================================

EARTH TAUGHT THE PLAYER TO BUILD FOR PEOPLE THEY UNDERSTOOD.

SPACE TESTS WHETHER THEY CAN BUILD RESPONSIBLY FOR BEINGS THEY DO NOT.

The Earth game asks:

Why do humans visit, remember, and return?

The space game asks:

Can the player recognize meaning without forcing the unknown into familiar
human categories?

This turns the expansion into a reasoning adventure inside a tycoon game.
